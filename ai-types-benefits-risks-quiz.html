Different Types of AI

Artificial intelligence is technology that allows computers to perform tasks that normally require human abilities such as recognising patterns, solving problems, understanding language or making predictions. Narrow AI, also called weak AI, is designed for one limited task, such as recommending a movie, recognising a face or filtering spam. Machine learning is a method that helps a system learn patterns from data instead of being programmed with every possible rule. Generative AI uses patterns learned from large collections of information to create new content such as text, images, music, audio or computer code. Artificial general intelligence (AGI) describes a still-theoretical system that could learn and perform a wide range of intellectual tasks at a human-like level, while superintelligent AI refers to a hypothetical future system that would exceed human intelligence in most areas.

⚠️ Part 2

Possible Disadvantages of AI

AI can produce incorrect or misleading answers, repeat bias found in its training data, threaten privacy, support scams and misinformation, and change or replace some types of work. Students may also become too dependent on AI and miss opportunities to practise thinking, writing and problem-solving for themselves. AI systems run in data centres filled with powerful computers that use substantial electricity and generate heat. Some data centres use large amounts of water in cooling systems, which can place additional pressure on local water supplies, especially in hot or water-stressed areas. The exact impact varies according to the size and location of the centre, its source of electricity, the type of cooling system and how often water is reused.

🌱 Part 3

Benefits of AI

When used carefully, AI can help people analyse large amounts of information, automate repetitive work and identify patterns that humans might miss. Environmental uses include monitoring forests, predicting severe weather, improving energy efficiency and helping farmers use water or fertiliser more precisely. In medicine, AI can support healthcare professionals by helping to examine medical images, identify possible warning signs, organise records and prioritise urgent cases; however, qualified professionals must still review important decisions. In education, AI can create interactive activities, provide timely feedback, adjust practice to a learner’s needs, support translation and accessibility, and make lessons more engaging. It should support—not replace—the judgement, care and creativity of teachers and other professionals.

🛡️ Part 4

Using AI Responsibly

Responsible AI requires shared action. Users should check important claims, protect personal information, disclose meaningful AI use and avoid submitting work they do not understand. Schools and workplaces need clear rules, human supervision, digital-literacy education and fair procedures for handling errors or bias. Governments can require safety testing, privacy protections, transparent reporting, environmental standards and accountability when automated decisions harm people. Technology companies can train smaller and more efficient models, power facilities with lower-carbon energy, report energy and water use, reuse treated or non-drinking water where appropriate, recover waste heat, and choose cooling systems suited to local conditions. Alternatives such as closed-loop liquid cooling, direct-to-chip cooling, immersion cooling, air cooling and locating facilities in cooler climates may reduce freshwater use, although each option has its own energy, cost and environmental trade-offs.
