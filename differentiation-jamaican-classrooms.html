Differentiation in Jamaican Classrooms

Practical strategies for helping learners with diverse educational needs participate, understand and demonstrate learning—even when classes are large, resources are limited, and a formal diagnosis or specialist support is not yet available.

Start Here

The learner is already in your classroom.

Some students arrive in Jamaican classrooms with needs that have never been formally identified. Others may have a diagnosis but little classroom support. A teacher may be expected to respond without an aide, specialist, psychologist, specialised equipment or a separate programme.

The question is not only “What does this child have?”
The more useful classroom question is: “What is getting in the way of learning, and what can I change?”
Talk with someone beside you.
Think of a learner who was physically in the classroom but was not meaningfully participating in the lesson. What was the learner doing? What was the class doing? What support was missing?
Jamaican Classroom Reality

Differentiation has to work in the classroom teachers actually have.

This workshop does not assume a small class, unlimited resources or a specialist beside every teacher. The activities ask what can be done within real teaching conditions while keeping the learner connected to meaningful curriculum.

Large classes
One teacher may be responsible for many learners with very different levels of readiness.
Unidentified needs
A learner may show significant difficulty without having a formal diagnosis.
Literacy gaps
A student can reach upper primary or secondary school while reading far below the level of the assigned text.
Limited specialist support
Teachers may need practical classroom strategies while referrals or assessments are unavailable or delayed.
Curriculum pressure
Teachers still have content to cover and students may be preparing for PEP, CSEC or other assessments.
Behaviour concerns
Learning difficulty, frustration, sensory overload or executive-function needs can sometimes appear first as “indiscipline.”
Important: Teachers do not need to diagnose students in order to respond to observed learning needs. Support can begin with what the teacher sees, hears and notices in the classroom.
A Simple Thinking Framework

Look at the learner, task, barrier and adjustment.

1. LearnerWhat can this learner already do? What strengths, interests and needs do you notice?
2. TaskWhat is the actual learning objective? What must every learner understand or demonstrate?
3. BarrierWhat part of the lesson is creating unnecessary difficulty?
4. AdjustmentWhat can change without removing the core learning?
Case Study 1 • Form 1 English Language

When “misbehaviour” begins with a reading task

Meet Dwayne

Dwayne talks, walks around and avoids the worksheet.

A Form 1 class is asked to read a two-page passage independently and answer eight comprehension questions. Dwayne looks at the page for several minutes, begins talking to the boy beside him and eventually walks across the room. The teacher tells him, “Every day is the same thing with you. Sit down and do your work.”

Later, the teacher discovers that Dwayne is struggling to decode much of the passage.

Decision 1
If the learning objective is identifying the main idea, which response is the strongest starting point?
Yes. If reading fluency is not the skill being assessed, the teacher can change the route into the content while preserving the learning goal.
Ask whether the response helps Dwayne reach the learning objective—or simply removes him from the learning.
Your turn. Name two low-cost ways Dwayne could still work on identifying the main idea.

The teacher has explained all six steps verbally and tells Shanice that she needs to pay better attention.

Decision 2
Which adjustment can the teacher use without diagnosing Shanice?
Correct. Classroom support can respond to observed attention, memory and organisation needs without the teacher making a diagnosis.

Case Study 4 • Dyslexia & Literacy

She knows the answer when she can say it.

Meet Rochelle

Rochelle contributes thoughtful answers during Social Studies discussions. When she must read several paragraphs and write extended responses, she works slowly, loses her place and produces much less than she can explain orally.

Decision 4
If the objective is explaining causes and effects, which approach best preserves the learning?
Correct. The support addresses literacy access while keeping Rochelle engaged with the same core concept.
Focus on preserving the concept being taught rather than replacing it with unrelated work.
Case Study 5 • Behaviour & Regulation

“He is disruptive.” What else do we know?

Meet Malik

Malik calls out, jokes with peers and leaves his seat during long independent written tasks. During practical demonstrations, debates and activities where he can move and contribute, he is engaged and often gives useful answers.

The Question Teachers Ask

“But I have 40 students.”

“I cannot teach one lesson for the class, another for the child with autism, another for the child who cannot read and another for the child who cannot sit still.”

That concern is reasonable. Differentiation should not require forty separate lesson plans. Often one change in lesson design improves access for several learners at the same time.

Instead of A long verbal explanation with all instructions given at once.
Try Short explanation + steps on the board + worked example + quick partner check.
Instead of One long written task for everyone with no checkpoints.
Try Chunk the task, check after each section and give a model of the expected response.
Instead of Assuming quiet sitting is the only evidence of attention.
Try Purposeful movement, response cards, paired talk, mini-whiteboards or brief active checks.
Instead of Sending one learner to the back with unrelated work.
Try Keep the same learning goal and change the level of support, text access, response method or pacing.
Strategy Bank

Low-cost strategies teachers can use tomorrow

Dyslexia & significant reading difficulty
Chunk text; pre-teach vocabulary; read key sections aloud; allow paired reading; reduce unnecessary copying; use larger spacing and clear formatting; provide sentence starters; let students explain ideas orally before writing; use audio where available; and separate the skill being assessed from reading speed when reading fluency is not the objective.
Autism, predictability & sensory needs
Use clear routines; show the sequence of the lesson; warn students before changes; reduce unnecessary ambiguity; offer structured choices; use visual instructions; allow processing time; consider noise, lighting and seating; and create predictable routes for asking for help or taking a brief regulation break.
ADHD, attention & executive-function needs
Give one step at a time when needed; write steps visibly; use checklists; shorten teacher-talk segments; provide purposeful movement; use quick feedback; break larger assignments into checkpoints; organise materials consistently; use timers where helpful; and check that the learner knows what “finished” looks like.
Behaviour, regulation & disengagement
Look at what happens before the behaviour; consider task difficulty, boredom, embarrassment, communication, peer attention, sensory load, fatigue and emotional regulation; teach replacement behaviours; reinforce successful participation; provide choices where possible; and avoid assuming that all refusal is deliberate defiance.
Very low literacy in a secondary classroom
Keep the learner connected to age-appropriate concepts while adjusting how information is accessed. Use oral explanation, images, diagrams, key vocabulary, paired work, short accessible text, teacher read-aloud, matching, sequencing, labelling, demonstrations and structured response frames rather than giving unrelated primary-level work.
Quick Check

Myth or accurate?

“A teacher should wait for a formal diagnosis before adjusting instruction for a learner who is clearly struggling.”
Correct. A diagnosis may guide support, but teachers can respond immediately to observable learning barriers.
Consider what the teacher can change based on classroom evidence without attempting to diagnose the learner.
“Differentiation means making the lesson easier for students with Special Educational Needs.”
Correct. Differentiation changes access, support, pacing, grouping, materials or response options while preserving meaningful learning.
The aim is access—not automatically lower expectations.
“A strategy designed for a learner with Special Educational Needs may also improve learning for other students.”
Correct. Clear steps, modelling, visuals, chunking, movement and multiple response methods often strengthen instruction for many learners.
Think about whether good instructional design benefits only students with identified needs.

Final Case

The student at the back of the room

You enter a classroom during a Science lesson. Most students are examining a diagram, discussing the topic and answering questions.

THE CLASS
Learning about the human digestive system, discussing vocabulary and completing a labelled diagram.
THE STUDENT AT THE BACK
Colouring an unrelated worksheet alone.

A member of staff explains:

“He has Special Educational Needs. He cannot really do what the rest of them are doing.”

Is the learner included—or merely present?

Discuss: What would you need to know before accepting the conclusion that the learner cannot participate?
Now redesign the situation.
How could the learner participate meaningfully in the same Science topic with appropriate support?
