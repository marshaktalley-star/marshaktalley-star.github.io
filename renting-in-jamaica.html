01 / My experience

I was still trying to settle in.

Returning to Jamaica as an adult is more than getting yourself and your things here. It involves rebuilding routines, learning and navigating unfamiliar systems while trying to make a house feel like a home.

I paid six months’ rent in advance because I wanted somewhere stable while I settled into this next chapter.

However, renting was not my first choice. I never wanted to contend with a landlord’s ego or anyone who had the ability to put me out of my safe space, so I initially wanted to purchase my house before even moving back. That proved to be neither practical nor wise.

Within the very first months, I was dealing with disputes about utility charges, access to the property, gardening arrangements, viewings and how I was spoken to when I asked questions. From the way she responded, it was clear to me that the owner believed she or whomever she chose could do whatever they liked because she owned the house and I was just a tenant. She told me I should remember my place.

Now, I know off the bat you may be thinking I did something wrong. Nope. I simply asked to know when the gardener was coming and that the caretaker not enter the house without my prior approval or when my daughter was there alone.

July–August 2026 · Paperwork and utilities I asked for confirmation of the funds paid, a digital copy of the executed lease and correction of the blank initial term to 12 months. I also asked for electricity charges covering my occupancy and for boundaries around the caretaker’s visits. A contract was later delivered, but the PDF I have shared for this account still has the initial term blank.

October 2026 · Maintenance and a viewing A disagreement about gardener access became a reprimand about my status as a tenant. Later, a visit described as an inspection of the grounds appeared to me to be a viewing, even though we had agreed that prospective buyers could view only after six months. During that visit, the man filmed my daughter and me while we were in the house, before I could close the curtains. He also filmed the house and my car.

6 November 2026 · Boundaries and escalation After another visitor entered the yard, I raised concerns about notice, recording and security. The landlord replied that the property was hers, that the person she sent did not take instructions from me and that I should remember my place as a tenant. I asked that all future correspondence be sent by email and shared my email address again. I then removed the landlord and realtor from the WhatsApp group I had created for effective communication.

7 November 2026 · A notice delivered A man drove into my driveway, sounding his horn, and identified himself as being from the courts. He filmed my daughter, me, my car and the house before leaving a letter at the door. I did not engage in a dialogue or confrontation. I found the encounter intimidating. The notice was dated 6 November, signed in the landlord’s name and requested possession for the owner’s use and occupation. It was not a judge’s possession order.

Let me be clear: I owe this landlord no money, I have caused no damage to the property and I am not a nuisance to the community. Asking for proper notice and setting boundaries did not make me a subordinate who needed to know her place.

The landlord did not follow our agreement about notice and access. The lease required 24 hours’ notice, with one unannounced inspection each year. The first visit was a viewing, with the visitor showing the house to someone on a video call. Even treating it as an inspection would not excuse the other visits without proper notice.

The realtor told me the house would be removed from the market after I signed, yet it remained advertised. Our agreement also placed buyer viewings after the first six months of my year-long tenancy, but people were sent to view it before then. Now, despite those sale discussions, viewings and public listings, the notice says the house is required for the owner’s use and occupation. That discrepancy needs legal advice before deciding how to respond.

“This is Jamaica” does not mean anything goes. Owning the house does not put a landlord above the law, and renting it does not take away a tenant’s rights. The lease, the stated reason for ending it and the legal process all matter.

Random people in the yard is a security risk.

I have a small media presence, so having unannounced people I do not know recording my daughter, me, my car and where I live is problematic. Even if I had no presence at all, it would still be unnerving. What if someone has ill intentions?

I live in a gated community, yet people authorised by the homeowner can still arrive without arrangements being made with me. That has left me concerned about our safety and security. That is why it was necessary to contact the homeowners association, the security coordinator, and file a police report as necessary.

Before I moved to Jamaica, a woman was found murdered in her home in a gated community. That stayed with me. [11] Based on the pattern I have seen here, I can see how people can gain access without the person living in the home knowing. If a homeowner authorises people you know nothing about to come to the house, that can be dangerous.

I also had two men turn up saying they were “Digicel” workers. They arrived in unmarked vehicles, had no identification and were walking around the property. I did need repairs, but when I contacted Digicel, they denied sending anyone to my house. The men said they were contractors, not directly employed by Digicel.

In retrospect, I wondered whether the homeowner could have sent them. I do not know who sent them or who authorised their entry. That is the problem. Who would know? The person living in the house needs to know who is coming and why.

I am sharing this because someone else returning to Jamaica may be about to sign a lease or already dealing with something similar. I wanted somewhere stable. I am now looking for another home. These are the things I would want another returnee to know before signing.

02 / Before you commit

Ask the questions before you pay.

When you are trying to organise a move back, finding somewhere to live can feel like one more thing you need to get sorted. I understand wanting to get it done. Still, ask the questions and get the answers in writing before you pay. A beautiful house in a gated community does not tell you what living there will be like.

03 / The agreement

Put it in the lease. All of it.

If something matters to how you will live in the house, do not leave it as a conversation you hope everyone remembers. Put it in the lease. This checklist covers what I would want clear before signing; it is not a list of terms the law requires in every lease. Have an independent Jamaican attorney check the wording and whether it can be enforced.

Include Make the wording specific
Parties and property Full names, service addresses, authorised agent, exact property and areas included; permitted occupants, pets and parking.
Term and dates Start and end dates, agreed duration, renewal process and any break clause. Distinguish advance rent from the length of the tenancy.
Rent and payments Amount, currency, due date, approved payment method, receipts, any conversion method, lawful increases and fees.
Deposits and advance rent Each sum separately; permitted deductions, evidence required, refund arrangements and treatment of unused prepaid rent if the tenancy ends early.
Inventory and condition A dated, jointly acknowledged inventory and condition schedule, supported by photographs and videos, meter readings, key counts and existing defects.
Repairs and bills Responsibility for structural work, routine repairs, utilities, old balances, water, sprinklers, garden care, HOA fees and response arrangements.
Entry and inspections Purposes, frequency, notice, method of arranging appointments, visitor identification and a narrowly defined emergency process. Review any unannounced-inspection exception before accepting it.
Gardeners and caretakers Named workers or an agreed approval process; working days and time windows, areas of access, keys, gates, equipment routes, parking needs and who resolves disagreements.
Photography and recording Agreed limits on photographing occupants, belongings, vehicles and interiors; purpose, recipients, use, retention and consent arrangements for marketing footage.
Sale and viewings Actual sale status, any agreed withdrawal, when showings may begin, appointment arrangements and what happens if ownership changes. Obtain advice on the tenancy’s protection.
Notice and disputes Service addresses, written communication channels, applicable notice and termination terms, dispute contacts, move-out inspection and handover.
Signatures and schedules Every party’s signature and date; attached rules, inventory and agreed amendments. Keep a complete signed copy.
04 / Everyday boundaries

Yes, even the gardener needs an arrangement.

Ask about the gardener and caretaker before you move in. Find out what they have been told, and make sure you have all agreed to the same arrangement. Something that seems small beforehand can become a conflict about your privacy and safety later.

  • Agree who attends, their role, usual hours and how substitutions will be notified.
  • Identify the approved route for mowers and equipment. Establish how much warning you need to move a vehicle and where you can park.
  • Define access to the front garden, backyard, storage and interior. Agree on notice for changes and the process for genuine emergencies.
  • Record who holds keys or access credentials. Ask how your privacy and household security will be protected.
  • Ask the landlord to give workers the same written arrangements. A tenant should not have to renegotiate the boundaries on the doorstep.

Have the clause professionally drafted. It should coordinate the landlord’s legitimate maintenance needs, your occupation of the home and the HOA’s security procedures. Do not assume that every visit needs your express consent, or that ownership permits unrestricted entry: the purpose, agreement and applicable law matter.

05 / One clear record

Keep the communication in writing.

One thing I am glad I did was create a WhatsApp group with the landlord and realtor. I wanted us all to receive the same information at the same time. If you can, do this from the beginning. Put repair requests, questions and agreed visits in the group, or use email. After a phone conversation, send a message confirming what was discussed.

Share the move-in inventory, condition photographs and videos, meter readings, defects, payment confirmations and agreed repair dates. Ask both parties to acknowledge them. Keep large original files in a private folder and share a secure link if necessary. A WhatsApp message is not a substitute for a signed lease amendment or a legally required method of serving notice.

  • After a call: send a short written summary and invite correction. Silence is not necessarily agreement.
  • For a visit: record the names, purpose, date, time window and areas of access.
  • For an incident: record what happened, when, who attended, witnesses and the exact words you can recall. Separate observation from suspicion.
  • For evidence: export chats with media, save emails and notices, and download camera recordings before they expire. Keep original files unchanged and make a separate working copy.
  • For continuity: if WhatsApp becomes abusive and you block someone, preserve the record first and provide an alternative written channel for essential tenancy communication and notices.

Back up records privately in at least two places. Note any camera-clock or time-zone difference. If the HOA holds CCTV or entry logs, promptly request preservation for the relevant period; it may need to release them to police rather than directly to you.

06 / If it happens to you

Stay composed. Get support. Keep the evidence.

You may be upset. You may have every reason to be. Still, say what you need to say, keep it brief and do not get pulled into a quarrel. You do not need to stand at your gate explaining yourself over and over. Keep your composure, get somewhere safe if you need to and let the written record speak for itself.

  1. Put immediate safety first. If you feel unsafe, remain in a secure place and contact security or police. Do not physically confront, obstruct or try to remove a visitor yourself. For an emergency in progress or immediate danger, call 119 . [5]
  2. Ask for identification and purpose. Ask a visitor to identify themselves, whom they represent and what they are delivering or inspecting. Verify a claimed official role through an independently obtained office number. Accepting a document does not itself mean accepting its claims; keep it and obtain advice. Do not obstruct lawful service.
  3. Notify the HOA and security coordinator in writing. Give the factual timeline, relevant clips and names where known. Ask for an incident record, preservation of entry logs/CCTV and clarification of visitor protocols. Ask security to apply community rules; do not expect it to decide the tenancy dispute or prevent lawful court process.
  4. Preserve evidence of recording. Note who filmed, where, what appeared to be captured and any objections you made. Ask in writing about the purpose, recipients and intended use. Seek advice if footage is shared or threats are made. Do not publicly post unredacted material in response.
  5. Make a non-emergency police report where appropriate. Describe the conduct and why it concerns you. Bring the timeline, messages, footage and notice. Ask for the report/reference number and reporting officer’s details. Police can assess safety or possible criminal conduct; a report does not determine whether a tenancy notice is valid. The DPP advises contacting or attending a local police station for non-emergencies. [6]
  6. Seek tenancy advice promptly. Take the full lease, payment statement, notice and supporting evidence to the Rent Services Unit/Rent Assessment Board and an attorney or legal clinic. Ask about coverage under the Act, the stated ground, notice dates and your agreed term.
  7. Keep meeting your obligations. Do not stop paying rent, use the deposit as rent or make unilateral deductions without specific advice. Account for prepaid rent. Keep proof of all payments and follow any court directions.
  8. Choose your route with advice. Options may include a written clarification request, assisted dispute resolution, a negotiated move with agreed financial terms, or defending possession proceedings. If court papers arrive, meet their deadlines even while negotiating.

A brief boundary message

Please confirm in writing the visitor’s name, role, purpose, proposed date and time window, and areas requiring access. Please arrange attendance in accordance with our tenancy agreement and the community’s security procedures. I will address tenancy matters through written communication. Please confirm any proposed recording and its intended use beforehand.

Adapt this to your agreement. This message is not a legal notice or a blanket refusal of lawful access.

If you decide to leave

Get the arrangement in writing: departure date, unused prepaid rent, deposits, utility reconciliation, agreed deductions, joint inspection, key handover and payment dates. Keep your own dated move-out photographs and meter readings. Obtain advice before signing a document that describes a landlord-requested departure as your voluntary early termination or waives claims.

07 / The legal distinction

I needed to know what that letter actually meant.

When someone arrives saying they are from the courts, it can make a letter feel like a decision has already been made about you. I needed to check that. Jamaica’s Supreme Court explains that a landlord may serve a notice to quit before bringing a recovery-of-possession claim. A notice signed by a landlord is different from a court summons, an order for possession or a warrant. A deliverer saying they are “from the courts” does not establish that a judge issued the document. [4]

Can “owner’s occupation” mean “I want to sell”?

For premises covered by the Rent Restriction Act, section 25(1)(e)(i) addresses a reasonable requirement for residential occupation by the landlord or specified connected people. The court must consider reasonableness and the relevant balance of hardship. Sale alone is not a general possession ground under section 25. Section 25(1)(d) addresses the different situation where a tenant first gives notice and the landlord acts in reliance on it. [1]

In J Wray & Nephew Ltd v Levy [2020] JMSC Comm 9, paragraphs 17–18, the Supreme Court rejected the attempt to treat selling with vacant possession as the landlord’s business use. This was a commercial tenancy decision whose reasoning also discusses residential protection. In Barrett v Dale [2020] JMCA Civ 13, paragraphs 65–73, the Court of Appeal confirmed that the claimed occupation ground must be established by evidence, not merely written in the notice. [2] [3]

Sale listings, viewing messages and an agent’s statements can be relevant evidence when questioning an occupation claim. They do not conclusively prove a false statement: intentions may change. Get advice about your facts and whether the property is covered by the Act or lawfully exempt.

Does the notice have to fit the tenancy dates?

Do not assess a notice by its heading alone. Ask an adviser to check the reason, date served, required departure date, rental periods, fixed term, break clauses and applicable law. The Rent Board’s guidance explains that residential notices ordinarily need to align with a full rental month and that an existing written lease matters. Exceptions and individual circumstances require review. [7]

Can the landlord simply force you out?

For controlled premises, section 27 prohibits forcible removal or acts calculated to interfere with quiet enjoyment or compel departure except under a competent court’s possession order or judgment. Seek urgent assistance if locks are changed, utilities are interfered with or threats arise. A disagreement about a notice should be addressed through advice and lawful process, not a confrontation. [1]

Use this guide to ask informed questions. It is general information, not a determination that a particular notice is valid or invalid. Confirm the property’s legal status, the current law and your response with an appropriate adviser. Do not ignore court documents.

09 / Evidence & further reading

Keep your originals. Be careful what you share.

Retain the complete unedited evidence privately for advisers, police or the court. Before publishing footage, review it for faces, voices, number plates, house numbers, access arrangements and identifying details about children. Make a redacted publication copy while preserving the original. Embedding a video elsewhere does not redact its original YouTube upload.
