Returning to Jamaica

I came home to Jamaica. Now I am learning how to live here again.

I left Jamaica at 14 and recently returned after decades living overseas. I did not come back to a family house, inherited land or relatives waiting to help me carry the cost of starting over. I came back as a single mother and the sole provider for my household. I am rebuilding in my homeland with my will, my skills and my resilience.

This is not a holiday. Living here as an adult is a whole different experience. It requires a level of patience I do not always have.

For full clarity, this page is not about bashing Jamaica. I love being home again. I am sharing what I am learning so that others who want to return can make informed decisions, avoid unnecessary and painful pitfalls, and hopefully have an easier transition home.

This is what coming home really looks like

I love being home. That does not mean I am going to pretend everything works well.

There is so much I love about being back in Jamaica. There are also things that frustrate me, things that make absolutely no sense to me, and things I am having to relearn after decades away.

Some days Jamaica works my last nerve. Other days, somebody shows up with exactly the information, connection or kindness I needed. Both are part of coming home.

It is not about learning to beat the system. It is about understanding how things actually work.
What coming home has felt like for me

I have had some hard lessons since returning to Jamaica, and you will read about some of them here. But that is not the whole story. I have also had a tremendous amount of help.

I have literally made more contacts here in Jamaica in weeks than I did in the UK in three years. People have not gate-kept important information from me. They have put me on, told me who to call, where to go, what to ask, and sometimes helped me get into rooms I would not have known how to enter on my own. My experience so far is that when people realise you are ambitious, driven and serious about what you are trying to build, some people genuinely want to help you move forward.

My aunt told me I would regret moving back to Jamaica, and yes, there are moments when I ask myself, “Marsha, did you make the right choice?” So far, though, what I am realising is that I can make it here. I just need to learn HOW to make it here.

Living in Jamaica is not the same as living in the US or the UK, and that is okay. I do not need Jamaica to operate like somewhere else. I need to learn how to live here.

Returning Resident concessions, step by step

This is the part people keep asking me about.

I was asked again yesterday how to get the Returning Resident letter and concessions, so I want this plainly on the page. The concession is handled by the Jamaica Customs Agency, not by your shipper, your broker or somebody's friend who "knows the process."

1
Check that you qualify.

For the standard Returning Resident category, Jamaica Customs currently says a Jamaican national age 18 or older must have lived overseas for the last three consecutive years and be returning to Jamaica to live permanently. There are separate provisions for returning students, spouses and deportees.

2
Register and apply through Jamaica Customs.

Use the Returning Residents process and submit the documents that apply to you. Customs lists identification, TRN, proof of local address and evidence of your years living overseas among the supporting records. A validated Bill of Lading or Airway Bill is also listed for the concession process.

3
Be ready to prove that you actually lived abroad.

Do not throw away your tax records, employment records or other evidence from overseas. Customs may use tax returns or transcripts, an employer separation letter, pension records, work permits, a notarised residence letter or other evidence to substantiate your time abroad.

4
Complete the Returning Resident review.

Jamaica Customs says returnees are subject to an interview. Approval depends on eligibility, the requirements being met and how quickly you respond when more information is requested. The concession should be claimed within six months after approval and is available once to a qualified returnee.

5
Know what the concession does, and what it does not do.

The Returning Resident concession can cover eligible personal and household effects and tools of trade. It does not make your motor vehicle duty-free. Jamaica Customs specifically says returning residents pay the full duties and taxes on a vehicle.

6
If you are bringing a vehicle, there is another process.

For a Returning Resident vehicle up to 10 years old, the Trade Board currently requires the Returning Resident pre/approval letter from Jamaica Customs, ownership of the vehicle for at least six months, the pre-shipment inspection certificate and the other vehicle documents before the import permit is approved. Do not confuse your Returning Resident approval with your vehicle import permit. They are not the same document.

What documents should I gather before leaving?
Keep passport copies, proof of overseas residence, tax/employment records, proof of Jamaican address, Returning Resident correspondence, shipping records, vehicle documents, invoices and permits in one digital folder and one physical folder. Save copies offline too.
What should be on my household inventory?
Create a detailed list of shipped items, quantities and reasonable values. Separate used household effects from new items. Keep receipts for recently purchased items when possible and photograph valuable belongings before the container is sealed.
Will everyday processes feel different from the UK or US?
Yes, and some of them may work your last nerve. I recently had to print a physical copy of an invoice, physically sign it, and physically take it to a local business in order to get paid. In my mind, the exact same invoice submitted electronically should be just as valid for an audit. To me, that process felt redundant.

I am learning, though, that constantly comparing every Jamaican process with how I did things in the UK or US will frustrate me. Ask what the process is, prepare for it, and where you can respectfully suggest a more efficient way, do that. Sometimes the quickest route is simply learning how things currently work here.

What else should I budget for besides shipping?
Broker fees, port/storage charges, transport, housing deposits, utilities, temporary transport, insurance, food and setup costs can overlap. Keep a contingency reserve rather than planning down to the last dollar.
Citizenship paperwork can become part of the move too

My daughter’s Jamaican citizenship took about a year.

I started my daughter's citizenship process while we were still living in the UK and followed up religiously. A couple of months before we moved to Jamaica, I found out that her name had been misspelled in the process. I then had to spend more money getting photographs done again and paying to resend documents to the Jamaican High Commission in London.

The frustration with the Jamaican High Commission in London had actually started much earlier. Before I even moved to Jamaica, only a couple of months after I first submitted my daughter's citizenship application and renewed my passport, I had already posted a Google review about the difficulty I was having getting updates and answers.

Why this review still matters to me

Looking back at this review, I wonder if it is the reason my daughter's application was delayed and later sent back to the UK, despite them having plenty of time to ensure we could pick it up locally. Did my criticism have anything to do with what happened later? I do not know, but it would not be the first time it did.

Regardless of the answer, this is quite possibly why others may not say anything. When you operate within a links culture, it is hard to complain about the person, company or government office from where you still need help. People may decide not to write the email, make the report, escalate the complaint or even get the law involved because they are afraid speaking up could make things worse for them.

That fear cannot become the reason we stay quiet. There should be systems strong enough that somebody can question a charge, challenge poor service, file a complaint or ask for accountability without wondering whether doing so will affect how they are treated afterwards. Accountability is necessary.

One thing that helped was keeping the same email thread going. I spoke with different people and emailed managers, but keeping the history together made it much easier to show what had already been said, what I had sent and what I was waiting for.

When we arrived in Jamaica in July, we went to PICA in person to check the progress. We were told to write a letter asking for the citizenship certificate to be collected locally when it was ready. Later, someone called and told me we had to come back to PICA and write a second letter because the first one had been written on the wrong paper. We got a ride there and did it again.

Yet when I followed up in August, I was told the certificate had been mailed back to the UK because our request to collect it in Jamaica had not been received in time. That was after I had sent several emails saying that we were now living in Jamaica, asked whether the requested letter could be sent electronically, sent one electronically anyway, and physically went to PICA twice.

I initially asked for a digital copy and was told that could not be done. After I asked both PICA and the Jamaican High Commission in London for the information I needed to make a formal complaint, someone sent me an electronic copy of my daughter's citizenship certificate and said the physical document would be sent back to the local office. I am still following up for the formal complaint information because this is exactly why keeping a paper trail matters.

For context: PICA currently says compliant Citizenship by Descent applications are processed within 90 working days under regular service. PICA also currently offers a 45-working-day expedited service for local applicants and London applicants. My experience took far longer than that.

At one point I was so frustrated I wondered whether I had pissed somebody off. I have no evidence that was why the document was sent back overseas. I simply could not understand why it happened after all the emails and the two visits to PICA.

Before you send it, sign it or follow somebody anywhere

Blind trust is not verification. Check what matters.

People who genuinely want to help you exist here in abundance. I have met many of them. That still does not mean I am sending my passport, TRN, permit, title or any other personal document willy-nilly because somebody says, "send it to me." I finally got my car. The process of getting it was another story.

I recorded this sitting in my car after dealing with all sorts of nonsense regarding my car and personal belongings. I was tired, sweaty and completely irritated at this point. The day before was a whole other ordeal, but I will save that story for another day.

Initially, I posted the video to Facebook, but quickly decided to delete it. I wanted to share it here with context. Instead of only sharing my frustration, I wanted to share what I learned from the process and give you practical ways to avoid some of what happened to me.

I hate posting anything negative online about my people or country, but the way many in Jamaica run businesses have got to change.

Imagine you spend yuh big big money fi ship yuh tings dem from farin. Only to end up in hell and powda house fi get them. It does not need to be this way, but the lack of accountability here is a problem.

We need systems in place that can hold Customs, wharf employees, brokers and the other people we depend on in this process accountable. Jamaica wants to become a developed nation, and this is one of the places where we can lead the way.

My experience is not the only story I have heard. People have shared their own shipping and vehicle-damage experiences with me. One man told me about a vehicle where part of the top was cut so it could fit in a container. Another told me about a car being badly scratched by the straps used to secure it. Those are accounts people told me directly, not incidents I independently verified, but they are another reason I now tell people to photograph and video the vehicle carefully before it is loaded and again before accepting it after shipping.

Almost two weeks later, I was still trying to get my belongings.

My personal belongings travelled in my car. After the car was inspected in Jamaica, the belongings were taken out and were not put back for my broker to clear everything together. I eventually got the car, with deep scratches and the antenna broken off, but not the belongings.

When my broker stopped responding, I called the company in Jamaica that had received the shipment from the UK. I was told that he had the Bill of Lading and that they could not simply reissue that legal document to me, even though the shipment was in my name. I was told I would need a police report first. Initially I was told I had to drive from St Ann to Newport to make that report in person. After I questioned that and spoke with someone higher up in the police, I was told I could make the report in St Ann, pay the required fee and wait for the formal report to be produced.

Here is the part that matters for you: the person acting as my broker came highly recommended by somebody in my community. She swore by him. He knew people and could get things done. Only afterwards did I learn that he was not a properly licensed Customs Broker. When everything is going smoothly, that distinction may not feel important. When something goes wrong and you need accountability, it becomes very important.

WarningA link is not a licence. Use the Jamaica Customs approved broker list before you appoint anybody. I learned that lesson the expensive way.

Ask how tightly your belongings are being packed.

A source who works in shipping told me directly that he used to pack customers' belongings as tightly as possible so more shipments could fit into the available container space and the company could make more money. He said he stopped because dealing with unhappy customers when belongings were damaged was not worth the headache.

I cannot tell you how widespread that practice is, and I am not going to pretend I can. I can tell you what someone inside the industry told me he personally did. Consolidated shipping itself is normal. My warning is to ask how your things will be packed, protected and secured, who is responsible for damage and what the insurance actually covers. Photograph your belongings before they leave you.

The bigger issue for me is accountability.

Jamaica runs heavily on “links.” Somebody knows somebody, somebody can give you a number, somebody can make a call. I have benefited from that generosity since coming home, and I am grateful for it. But links alone cannot be the entire system. When friends know friends, it can also become difficult to challenge poor service. A returning resident who knows nobody should still be able to understand the process, see what they are paying for, know who is responsible and have somewhere meaningful to go when something goes wrong.

My advice now: get names, fees and responsibilities in writing; keep every receipt and payment record; ask for the official document behind any charge you do not understand; and move important conversations out of phone calls and into writing. A “link” can help you find the right person. It should never replace a transparent process.

If something goes wrong, create a paper trail and escalate it.

Start with the company that actually handled the part of the process you are challenging. The shipping line, freight forwarder, terminal operator, broker and Jamaica Customs do not all perform the same role, so send the complaint to the organisation that was responsible for the disputed service. Ask for a written response, a claim or reference number, the company's claims procedure and any applicable insurance information. Attach the bill of lading, invoices, receipts, before-and-after photographs, collection documents, dates, names and repair estimates where relevant.

Shipping company, freight forwarder or terminal

Make the written claim directly first. Check the bill of lading or contract for notice deadlines and claim requirements. If you are unsure which local company handled the cargo, the Shipping Association of Jamaica directory can help identify shipping agents, freight forwarders and terminal operators.

Licensed Customs Broker

Complain to the broker in writing first. Jamaica Customs licenses Customs Brokers. If the problem concerns a broker's customs work or conduct and is not resolved, contact Jamaica Customs Customer Service and provide the broker's name and your supporting documents.

Jamaica Customs Agency

For a Customs service complaint, use the JCA General Queries or Complaints Form, email public.relations@jca.gov.jm, or call Customer Service at 876-948-7849 or toll-free 1-888-287-8667. JCA also lists quick.response@jca.gov.jm for general contact.

Consumer Affairs Commission

If you paid a Jamaican business for a product or service and the complaint remains unresolved, the CAC accepts formal consumer complaints. Its current contacts include complaints@cac.gov.jm, info@cac.gov.jm, 876-906-5425 and 876-619-4222.

PICA or citizenship service problem

Keep one email thread and ask for a case or ticket number. PICA currently lists 876-754-PICA (7422) and support@picahelpeu.freshdesk.com. Its REL-M customer relationship system is also designed to track issues. Citizenship services are handled at 8 Waterloo Road in Kingston and through Jamaican overseas missions.

If the problem crosses an overseas Mission and Jamaica

For a citizenship matter involving an overseas Jamaican Mission, copy the Mission and PICA so both sides can see the same chronology. The Jamaican High Commission in London is currently listed at jamhigh@jhcuk.com and +44 20 7823 9911. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Foreign Trade also lists consular@mfaft.gov.jm for Consular Affairs.

What should I check on the bill of lading?
Confirm your name, consignee details, destination, container information and vehicle details. Keep the final bill of lading accessible.
How quickly should I collect my shipment?
Arrival does not mean immediate release. In my experience, allowing roughly one to two weeks for clearance is sensible, although actual timing varies. Once you receive the release or collection message, move quickly to reduce avoidable storage charges.
What about pets, plants and restricted items?
Check permits before packing. Pets and plants can require prior approvals. Never assume something can simply travel inside the container.

03 Exporting your car from abroad + importing it into Jamaica

There are two sides to this journey: getting the car out, and getting it into Jamaica.

I shipped my car from the UK, so I had to deal with the export side there and the import side here. Do not let those become one blurry process in your head. Before the car leaves the country you are moving from, confirm its export requirements, ownership documents and Jamaica pre-shipment inspection requirements. On the Jamaica side, get the Returning Resident approval and Trade Board import permit sorted before the vehicle arrives. Returning Resident status does not make the vehicle duty-free.

Important: under the current Returning Resident motor-vehicle rules, the vehicle must have been in your possession for at least six months. Returning Resident status does not make the vehicle duty-free.
Permit

Approval → inspection → permit

For a Returning Resident vehicle, get the Jamaica Customs pre/approval letter, complete the required pre-shipment inspection, then submit the Trade Board permit application. The permit must be approved before the vehicle arrives in Jamaica.

Documents

Keep proof of ownership

Have the title/export record, seller invoice and evidence showing when you bought the vehicle.

Clearance

Prepare your TCC + broker

A TCC (Tax Compliance Certificate) is required for motor-vehicle clearance. The vehicle must also be cleared through a licensed Customs Broker.

The car process I wish somebody had laid out plainly

Export side first. Jamaica import side next.

1
Check the export rules where the car is now.

The country you are shipping from has its own vehicle-export and registration requirements. Deal with those there. Keep the title or registration/export certificate and proof that the vehicle belongs to you.

2
Check whether Jamaica requires pre-shipment inspection.

For used vehicles coming from designated countries, including the UK, USA, Canada, Japan, China, Singapore, Thailand and the UAE, the Trade Board currently requires pre-shipment inspection before import. Do not wait until the car is already on the water.

3
If you are using Returning Resident status, get that approval.

The Trade Board's Returning Resident vehicle application currently asks for the Jamaica Customs Returning Resident pre/approval letter. This is one reason the Returning Resident process needs to start early.

4
Apply for the Jamaica vehicle import permit.

The Trade Board application is made through JSWIFT. For a Returning Resident application, the current list includes the Returning Resident pre/approval letter, bill of sale or invoice, title/registration/export certificate, ID, TRN and the pre-shipment inspection certificate.

5
Do not ship first and hope the paperwork catches up.

Jamaica Customs says the import licence should be obtained before shipping arrangements are made. Returning Residents can currently import an eligible vehicle up to 10 years old if it has been owned for at least six months, but full duties and taxes remain payable.

6
Prepare for clearance in Jamaica.

Keep the import licence, title/export certificate, Bill of Lading, invoice and TCC together. Jamaica Customs says motor vehicles must be cleared through a licensed Customs Broker. Verify the broker yourself before handing over money or documents.

Trade Board: Returning Resident vehicle permit Trade Board: Pre-shipment inspection Jamaica Customs: vehicle import documents
If you later export a vehicle from Jamaica: Jamaica Customs currently says you need an export licence from the Trade Board before export. That is a separate process from bringing your car home to Jamaica.
What about the seller invoice?
Ask the dealer or seller for a properly completed invoice or bill of sale at the time of purchase. It is much easier than trying to reconstruct the paperwork months later.
Does a used UK vehicle need inspection?
Imported motor vehicles require a pre-inspection certificate from the country of export before importation. If you are shipping from the UK, confirm the current approved inspection process before the vehicle leaves.
Should I let someone import the vehicle in their name?
Do not use an informal arrangement simply to avoid paperwork. The importer named in the documents carries legal and financial significance. If someone proposes a structure you do not understand, get it explained in writing first.
04 Arrival + money

Once you land, start asking people what things are supposed to cost.

Learn what things normally cost. Use Jamaican dollars where it makes sense, keep payment records and ask people who actually live here where they shop.

People have been one of the best parts of coming home. Somebody knows somebody. Somebody gives you a number. Somebody tells you where to go, what to ask or who to speak to. I have benefited from that generosity more times than I can count. I just do not want to confuse having a link with having a transparent system.
Currency

Learn when JMD makes more sense

Repeated card conversion and foreign-currency pricing can quietly eat into your money through rates and fees.

Protection

Use traceable payments

For major purchases, favour methods that leave a clear record and may offer dispute protection.

Local life

Ask where people really shop

Farmers, market days and local suppliers can be very different from prices in tourist-heavy areas.

What should I know about cash and transfers?
Keep useful cash, but not all of your money in cash. Avoid irreversible transfers to people you do not know simply because you are being pressured to act quickly. Use your common sense even when someone makes a situation feel urgent.
What about transport while I wait for my car?
If route taxis are limited where you live, a short-term rental can be more practical and sometimes more economical than repeated private taxis or ride-hailing. Uber and other app-based options are available in parts of Jamaica, but availability can change by exact location. If you are going somewhere remote, think about the return trip before you leave and keep the number of a reliable local driver. See the Getting Around + Useful Apps section for the fuller transport and safety notes.
Where should I buy food?
Ask when farmers bring produce directly to your local market. PriceSmart can be useful for bulk purchases, but compare unit prices. Tourist-heavy areas such as Ocho Rios, Montego Bay and Kingston can feel especially expensive.
People + connections

Network. Meet people. Just be intentional about it.

Since moving back, I have been connected to a LOT of people. Some of those introductions have helped me solve problems, find opportunities and get into rooms I would not have known about otherwise. Jamaica can be very relationship-driven, and WhatsApp groups are everywhere.

That can be a beautiful thing. It can also become noise very quickly.

Every contact does not need to become a friendship. Every event does not need your attendance. Every WhatsApp group does not need your daily attention.

“I have literally made more contacts in Jamaica in weeks than I did in the UK in three years.”
That part surprised me.
01

Go where your interests are

Attend events that connect to the life, work or community you are actually trying to build. You do not have to be everywhere.

02

Use WhatsApp groups on purpose

They are useful for information, referrals, events and introductions. Mute the noisy ones. Leave the ones that add nothing. Protect your attention.

03

Follow up with the right people

If you have a good conversation, send the message. Save the number with a note so six weeks later you remember who the person is and where you met.

04

Do not confuse access with trust

Someone giving you a “link” does not automatically make that person, or the person they referred, trustworthy. Verify what needs verifying.

05

Let relationships be reciprocal

Networking should not feel like collecting people for what they can do for you. Share information. Make introductions when appropriate. Be useful too.

06

Keep some boundaries

You can be warm, open and connected without telling everybody everything. Be selective about who gets access to your plans, finances and personal life.

What I am learning: relationships matter here, but intentional relationships matter more. Build your circle. Show up. Talk to people. Take the number. Join the group. Then use your judgement. You do not need 500 contacts. You need good people you can actually call, and people who know they can call you too.

05 Getting around + useful local apps

Always secure your ride back home before you leave.

This was one of those things I learned very quickly. You can get taxis and, in some areas, use ride-hailing apps. I have even been able to get an Uber up into more remote places. The problem is not always getting there. Sometimes the bigger question is: how are you getting back?

What I would do

If a driver takes you somewhere that is harder to reach, ask whether they are available to collect you later. Save the number of a reliable local taxi driver. If you decide to arrange a return trip directly with a ride-hailing driver, remember that a trip arranged outside the app may not have the same in-app tracking or protections.

Local people can also be a huge help here. A driver who cannot collect you may know another driver who can. That has been part of my experience of Jamaica too. People will often give you the link rather than leave you to figure everything out alone.

Before you get into an Uber or other app-booked car, CHECK.

Do not get into a vehicle simply because someone says they are your driver. Match what is in the app first.

1. Match the licence plate Check the plate against the one shown in the app. If it does not match, do not get in.
2. Check the driver + vehicle Compare the driver's photo and the vehicle make/model with what the app shows.
3. Use the PIN + share your trip If your app offers ride PIN verification, use it and wait for the trip to verify before you leave. Share your live trip/location with someone you trust.

Uber's Jamaica safety guidance specifically tells riders to match the plate, vehicle and driver before entering, and its Verify Your Ride feature can require a four-digit PIN before the driver starts the trip.

How can I keep electricity costs under control?
AC can become expensive if it runs all day. Use blackout or thermal curtains, fans where comfortable, and keep windows closed during peak heat if the outdoor air is hotter than the inside. Open windows early morning, evenings or after rain when the outside temperature is actually cooler.

Can rainwater help?
Covered rainwater storage can support gardening, cleaning and other non-potable uses. Keep tanks covered and maintained so they do not become mosquito-breeding sites. Water intended for drinking or food preparation must be handled and treated safely.

Renting in Jamaica

Renting? Remember that it is your home while you are living there.

Arrangements that worked when a property was empty, used as an Airbnb or occupied by short-term guests may not work once somebody becomes a residential tenant.

Although people who have looked after a property for years may be accustomed to coming and going on their own schedules, it is important to establish boundaries.

Despite what many might believe, Jamaica has landlord-and-tenant protections. The Rent Restriction Act provides for a tenant who is meeting their obligations to peaceably and quietly occupy and enjoy the premises, while tenants must allow landlords or their agents reasonable access for inspection and necessary repairs.

Access for maintenance does not mean unrestricted access to your home.
Before you sign a leaseClarify access and maintenance.

Who has keys? Who is authorised to enter? How much notice will you receive? Who maintains the garden and property? When can they come? What happens if access needs to be changed?

My experience: when long-standing property access collided with my tenancy

I rented my home through a realtor and paid six months' rent in advance. The owner lives overseas and has long-standing people who have cared for the property through periods when it was empty, used as an Airbnb and occupied by short-term renters.

The caretaker had keys and did not want me to change the keypad code, telling me, “I am not going to just come in, you know.” On one occasion, however, he entered the house while I was out and only my daughter was home, without permission from either of us.

I also found that maintenance workers were accustomed to arriving on their own schedules. When I asked for agreed times so I could plan my day and protect my privacy, I was treated as though I was being difficult and should simply adjust to how things are done in Jamaica.

My lesson: long-standing arrangements between an owner and a caretaker, gardener or other worker do not automatically fit a residential tenancy. Clarify access, keys, notice and maintenance arrangements before you move in, and put them in writing where possible.

Look beyond the development

Do not only look inside the gates.

That same day, after passing another new development, something else hit me. There is a LOT of development happening in Jamaica and some of these house prices are through the roof. The development itself may be new and polished, but what is happening immediately outside of it?

If you are spending that kind of money, especially if you are buying from overseas, do not only ask to see the house. Do not only ask to see the development. Ask to see the surrounding area too.

If you cannot view it yourself, ask the realtor for current video of the approach road, the neighbouring communities and what you will actually pass when you leave the development. I do not mean another promotional video with the pool, gate and model house. I mean the road you will drive, the communities around you, the shops, the terrain and the infrastructure outside the development.

Ask for three views: the house itself, the development or immediate community, and the roads and surrounding areas outside it.

A new development can have beautiful roads, drainage, landscaping and infrastructure inside its boundaries. That does not automatically tell you what exists outside of it. You are not only buying a house. You are buying into what you will have to navigate every day to live there.

This is not me trying to discourage anybody from buying in Jamaica. I came home because I want to live here. I want us to come home, make informed decisions, settle well and actually be happy here. That means seeing the real picture before we put down our money.

What I noticed that day

This was after I passed a new development and started thinking about the difference between what may be improved inside a development and what exists immediately outside of it.

“Gated” does not automatically mean safe. “Ungated” does not automatically mean unsafe.

Do not buy the stigma. Look at the actual community.

There can be a stigma around older or ungated Jamaican communities: that they are dirty, run-down or unsafe. That is too simplistic. Some established communities have mature trees, larger lots, individuality, long-term neighbours and the Jamaican residential feel that some returnees are actually looking for.

Gated developments can offer newer homes, pools, gyms, landscaping, controlled entry and other conveniences. They can also experience security incidents, and the lifestyle can come with association fees and rules governing things such as exterior changes, parking, landscaping or even what colours a house may be painted.

Should I rent first or buy right away?
Renting first can give you time to learn traffic patterns, utility reliability, internet options, noise, shopping, flooding/drainage, commute times and the personality of different communities before making a long-term commitment. Buying immediately may make sense if you already know the area well, have financing or cash organised and have done appropriate property due diligence. Neither route is automatically better.
What should I tell a real-estate agent before we start?
Give them your preferred area, realistic monthly rent or purchase range, absolute maximum, number of bedrooms, must-have features, deal-breakers and whether you want gated, established, rural, town-centre or resort-style living. Ask them not to send properties above your ceiling unless you specifically request comparisons.
How do I verify a realtor before I send personal documents?
Search the Real Estate Board of Jamaica's public register by the person's name, licence number or registration number. Check that the registration/licence is current and that the person is connected to the dealer they say they represent. If someone asks for your TRN, ID or other personal documents, ask why the information is required and use an official business channel where possible. A legitimate due-diligence request is not the same thing as sending documents blindly.

What should I verify before driving to a property viewing?
Confirm the appointment, access and keys. Ask whether your agent or the listing agent has recently seen the property. If the area is unfamiliar or rural, ask about the road surface, steep hills, flooding, narrow roads and whether an ordinary car can reasonably reach it. Ask for current video of the house, the development or immediate community, and the surrounding roads and areas outside it. What should I check before renting?
Test water pressure, internet availability, mobile signal, AC units, appliances, doors, windows and security features. Ask who pays utilities and maintenance, what is included in the rent, how deposits are handled, whether there are community fees or rules, and what happens when something breaks. Ask who has keys, gate remotes or access codes, and agree how landlord, caretaker and maintenance access will be handled. Photograph the condition of the property when you move in.
What should I check before buying?
Beyond the appearance of the house, investigate title and legal ownership, boundaries/survey information, property taxes, community or maintenance fees, access roads, drainage/flooding, water storage, electricity, internet, structural condition, insurance considerations and any restrictions that affect how you can use or alter the property. Use qualified Jamaican legal and property professionals for the formal checks.
How should I compare gated and ungated communities?
Visit at different times of day. Talk to people who actually live there. Look at lighting, pedestrian activity, road condition, drainage, access to shops and transport, noise, trees and shade, lot size, privacy, community rules and actual security practices. If you are looking for mature trees and that real Jamaican feel, a gated, resort-style community with cookie-cutter houses and an association telling you what colour you can paint your house may not be it. On the other hand, if controlled entry, newer amenities and a more uniform community are what make you comfortable, that may be exactly what you want.

Accent, belonging + coming home
“I know when to speak patois. I am not going to perform it to gain respect around here!”
🎧 A real conversation from my return

Coming home should not require proving that you are Jamaican.

One of the more unexpected parts of returning has been thinking about when I speak patois, when I use Standard English, and how people sometimes respond to the accent I brought home with me.

In this private conversation, I was talking to a local driver who advised me to chat patois to get better prices and treatment in certain situations. I understand what he means, but I speak patois when it comes naturally to me, not when I am forced. I should not have to switch up how I speak in order to receive fair treatment, a fair price or basic respect. I know that is typical overseas, and I am aware of code switching, but I am not going to do it by coercion in the place of my birth.

My perspective: Jamaicans who remained on the island are not unfamiliar with Jamaicans living a farin, so returning home with a different accent or a little twang is no surprise to anybody. Our little accents do not make us less Jamaican. A returning resident should not have to cuss someone out, exaggerate patois, or prove themselves to be treated properly.
Why this matters for returning residents
Yes, people read your accent before they find out about you. They assume all sorts just because you sound like a tourist. While code-switching may be natural communication for many, including me, it can feel like pressure to perform familiarity so that you are not overcharged, dismissed or treated as an outsider. As a returnee, it is important to learn from the locals. Ask what things normally cost and build relationships with them. Of course, be selective like you would anywhere else, but do not feel that you have to put on a front to be accepted.

Medication + chronic conditions

If you rely on regular medication, sort this early.

  1. Bring enough medication for the transition. Do not wait until you are almost out.
  2. Bring your medical records. Keep your medication list, dosages, diagnosis information and recent relevant test results.
  3. Find a Jamaican doctor. Take your records and current medication so the doctor can review your condition and issue local prescriptions where appropriate.
  4. Get your TRN. You will need it for an NHFCard application.
  5. Apply for an NHFCard if your condition qualifies. A registered doctor must certify the condition.
  6. Check whether your medication is subsidised. Use the NHF benefits search because not every drug is covered.
  7. Take your prescription and NHFCard to a participating pharmacy. You pay the remaining balance after the approved subsidy.
  8. Use Drug Serv if it fits your care. Public patients can access NHF Drug Serv pharmacy locations at hospitals and selected health centres.
  9. Ask about availability before you run out. Your exact medication may not always be stocked, so discuss appropriate alternatives with your doctor or pharmacist.

What should I know about public healthcare?
Jamaica maintains a no-user-fee policy for qualifying public patients in public facilities. That does not mean every medicine, test or private service is automatically free. Many people still keep private insurance or an emergency medical fund.
Should I apply for NIS?
NIS is Jamaica’s compulsory contributory social-security programme for people in insurable employment. Generally, people ages 18 to 70 who are gainfully occupied, including employed and self-employed persons, are required to be registered. Check your category and complete registration early.
What about a Jamaican driving licence?
Do not assume one rule fits every returning resident. Requirements can depend on your existing licence and the transaction you are completing. Confirm current requirements with the Island Traffic Authority and Tax Administration Jamaica. For a first learner's permit, the current process includes the Road Code Test and supporting documents such as certified passport photographs, proof of residence/identity, a medical certificate, TRN and proof of age.
What should I prepare for hurricane season?
Keep water, flashlights, charged battery banks, basic medicines, important phone numbers and a modest cash reserve. If your work depends on internet, test your backup data and power arrangements before a storm is approaching.
