Returning to Jamaica

Coming home is beautiful. The logistics can be a lot.

Start with the real experience

There is the official process. Then there is what actually happens.

Returning Resident information often tells you what form to complete. It does not always tell you what it feels like when your shipment arrives, the broker is unclear, the internet does not work, or you realise you have been losing money through everyday currency conversion.

I built this page around both: the things that are formally required and the practical decisions that can save you time, money and unnecessary stress.

Prepare early. Ask more questions than you think you need to. Trust — but verify.
What coming home has felt like for me

I have had some hard lessons since returning to Jamaica, and you will read about some of them here. But that is not the whole story. I have also had a tremendous amount of help.

I have literally made more contacts here in Jamaica in weeks than I did in the UK in three years. People have not gate-kept important information from me. They have put me on — told me who to call, where to go, what to ask, and sometimes helped me get into rooms I would not have known how to enter on my own. My experience so far is that when people realise you are ambitious, driven and serious about what you are trying to build, some people genuinely want to help you move forward.

My aunt told me I would regret moving back to Jamaica, and yes, there are moments when I ask myself, “Marsha, did you make the right choice?” So far, though, what I am realising is that I can make it here. I just need to learn HOW to make it here.

Living in Jamaica is not the same as living in the US or the UK — and that is okay. I do not need Jamaica to operate like somewhere else. I need to learn how to live here.

01 Before you move

Get the Jamaican side of your life ready first.

A TRN, Returning Resident approval, local money and an organised document file will make almost every later step easier.

Start here

Confirm Returning Resident eligibility

Do not assume the concession applies automatically. Apply and get the approval process moving.

Identity

Get your TRN early

Your TRN comes up repeatedly in tax, vehicle, banking and other Jamaican processes.

Money

Build a JMD landing fund

Have Jamaican dollars available for local transactions, deposits, transport and the unexpected.

What documents should I gather before leaving?
Keep passport copies, proof of overseas residence, tax/employment records, proof of Jamaican address, Returning Resident correspondence, shipping records, vehicle documents, invoices and permits in one digital folder and one physical folder. Save copies offline too.
What should be on my household inventory?
Create a detailed list of shipped items, quantities and reasonable values. Separate used household effects from new items. Keep receipts for recently purchased items when possible and photograph valuable belongings before the container is sealed.
Will everyday processes feel different from the UK or US?
Yes — and some of them may work your last nerve. I recently had to print a physical copy of an invoice, physically sign it, and physically take it to a local business in order to get paid. In my mind, the exact same invoice submitted electronically should be just as valid for an audit. To me, that process felt redundant.

I am learning, though, that constantly comparing every Jamaican process with how I did things in the UK or US will frustrate me. Ask what the process is, prepare for it, and where you can respectfully suggest a more efficient way, do that. Sometimes the quickest route is simply learning how things currently work here.

What else should I budget for besides shipping?
Broker fees, port/storage charges, transport, housing deposits, utilities, temporary transport, insurance, food and setup costs can overlap. Keep a contingency reserve rather than planning down to the last dollar.
02 Shipping your belongings

Know who has your things before they leave the UK.

The shipping company is only part of the chain. The person handling your belongings on the ground in Jamaica matters just as much.

Consolidate

Ask about one container

If sending a car and household goods, compare the full cost of shipping them together versus separately.

Verify

Know your Jamaican broker

Get the name, contact information and role of whoever will handle clearance before you pay.

In writing

Demand a cost breakdown

Know what is included, what is not, when storage starts and who is responsible at each stage.

What should I check on the bill of lading?
Confirm your name, consignee details, destination, container information and vehicle details. Keep the final bill of lading accessible.
How quickly should I collect my shipment?
Arrival does not mean immediate release. In my experience, allowing roughly one to two weeks for clearance is sensible, although actual timing varies. Once you receive the release or collection message, move quickly to reduce avoidable storage charges.
What about pets, plants and restricted items?
Check permits before packing. Pets and plants can require prior approvals. Never assume something can simply travel inside the container.
03 Importing your car

Do the permit work before the vehicle is on the water.

Vehicle mistakes are expensive. Current Returning Resident guidance uses a six-month ownership threshold, and Returning Resident status does not make the vehicle duty-free.

Key correction: the current ownership requirement is at least six months before the Returning Resident vehicle permit application — not one year.
Permit

Apply before shipping

Complete the Trade Board vehicle import permit process before making final shipping arrangements.

Documents

Keep proof of ownership

Have the title/export record, seller invoice and evidence showing when you bought the vehicle.

Clearance

Prepare TCC + broker

Motor vehicles require a TCC for clearance and must be cleared through a licensed Customs Broker.

What about the seller invoice?
Ask the dealer or seller for a properly completed invoice or bill of sale at the time of purchase. It is much easier than trying to reconstruct the paperwork months later.
Does a used UK vehicle need inspection?
Current rules include pre-shipment inspection/sanitisation requirements for used vehicles shipped from the UK. Confirm the approved inspection process before the vehicle leaves.
Should I let someone import the vehicle in their name?
Do not use an informal arrangement simply to avoid paperwork. The importer named in the documents carries legal and financial significance. If someone proposes a structure you do not understand, get it explained in writing first.
04 Arrival + money

Once you land, local knowledge starts saving you money.

Use JMD intentionally, compare normal prices, keep payment records and learn quickly where residents actually shop.

Do not underestimate the value of people. “Links” can sound negative when you first hear it, but some of what I have experienced is simply people sharing information and opening doors instead of gate-keeping. Someone knows somebody. Someone tells you where to go. Someone gives you a number. That kind of help has made a real difference for me since I came home.
Currency

Use JMD where practical

Repeated card conversion and foreign-currency pricing can quietly eat into your money through rates and fees.

Protection

Use traceable payments

For major purchases, favour methods that leave a clear record and may offer dispute protection.

Local life

Ask where people really shop

Farmers, market days and local suppliers can be very different from prices in tourist-heavy areas.

What should I know about cash and transfers?
Keep useful cash, but not all of your money in cash. Avoid irreversible transfers to people you do not know simply because you are being pressured to act quickly. Use your common sense even when someone makes a situation feel urgent.
What about transport while I wait for my car?
If route taxis are limited where you live, a short-term rental can be more practical and sometimes more economical than repeated private taxis or ride-hailing. Ask residents what normal fares are before agreeing to regular transport arrangements.
Where should I buy food?
Ask when farmers bring produce directly to your local market. PriceSmart can be useful for bulk purchases, but compare unit prices. Tourist-heavy areas such as Ocho Rios, Montego Bay and Kingston can feel especially expensive.
05 Power + connectivity

Use the sun. Build backups. Verify every service visit.

If your work, income or peace of mind depends on electricity and internet, redundancy is not a luxury.

Sun

Use solar where it makes sense

Homeowners can price rooftop systems. Renters can use portable solar panels and power stations for essentials.

Internet

Keep a second connection

If you work online, keep mobile data or another backup available even when you have fixed broadband or Starlink.

Storm season

Plan backup power early

Battery storage, charged power banks and a properly used generator can make outages much easier to manage.

“The modem was already in the house. That did not mean I had working internet.”

My internet experience

Flow was recommended to me and there was already a modem in the house, but I still could not get a working connection. Even after bills were paid, unresolved issues prevented service and I was told a technician was not being sent for new accounts at that time.

Digicel customer service was better in my experience, but service was inconsistent. After one outage I was still waiting weeks later for a technician call-back, so I moved to Starlink. Starlink has been more dependable overall, though I still find it slow at times. I especially appreciate being able to reach support directly through the app.

What happened with the unexpected “Digicel technicians”?
A man called saying he was coming to check my Digicel internet and gave me his name and a colleague’s name. Because I live in a gated community, I gave those names to security so the men could be admitted. When they arrived, neither their clothing nor their vehicle displayed Digicel branding.

Afterwards, I called Digicel to discuss the visit and was told there was no record of Digicel sending anyone out and no work order showing that technicians had been assigned to my home. Understandably, that frightened me. Security confirmed that the men had checked out of the community.

I then called the person who had originally contacted me. He explained that they did not work directly for Digicel, but were subcontractors working through Konnex, and that jobs could be shared through a WhatsApp group for technicians in the area to pick up. I could not independently reconcile that explanation with what Digicel customer service had told me.

My takeaway: even when a caller knows details about your service problem, verify the visit. Ask the provider for the technician’s name, contractor company and work-order/reference number. If you live in a gated community, give security only verified names. If anything does not match, do not feel pressured to let someone into your home while you are trying to sort it out.
How can I keep electricity costs under control?
AC can become expensive if it runs all day. Use blackout or thermal curtains, fans where comfortable, and keep windows closed during peak heat if the outdoor air is hotter than the inside. Open windows early morning, evenings or after rain when the outside temperature is actually cooler.
What should renters consider for solar?
Portable solar panels paired with a battery power station can keep routers, laptops, phones and lights running without permanently altering the property. Calculate what you actually need to power and for how long before buying.
Can rainwater help?
Covered rainwater storage can support gardening, cleaning and other non-potable uses. Keep tanks covered and maintained so they do not become mosquito-breeding sites. Water intended for drinking or food preparation must be handled and treated safely.
06 Renting, buying + choosing where to live

Choose the home that fits your life — not somebody else’s commission.

Whether you rent first or buy immediately, be clear about your real budget, the kind of community you want, and the trade-offs you are actually willing to make.

A good local agent should help you work within your budget and priorities. Give them your comfortable range and your absolute ceiling before they start sending properties.
Gated Modern amenities + controlled access But still assess security, fees, rules and how much freedom you want.
Established community Mature trees + individuality + local character But inspect the specific road, property and neighbourhood rather than relying on stereotypes.
Your budget

Make the budget a boundary

Tell the agent what you can comfortably afford, not simply the largest number a lender says you qualify for. If most of the properties being shown are above your range, redirect the search.

Buying

Get mortgage pre-approval first

If you plan to finance the purchase, obtain a current mortgage pre-approval — or refresh an older one — before seriously viewing properties. That gives you a clearer ceiling and can make the process smoother when you find a house you want.

Agent fit

Work with someone who listens

Commission is part of real-estate transactions, so your interests and the agent’s incentives are not automatically identical. You want an agent who respects your limits instead of continually steering you toward a more expensive sale.

“Gated” does not automatically mean safe. “Ungated” does not automatically mean unsafe.

Do not buy the stigma. Look at the actual community.

There can be a stigma around older or ungated Jamaican communities — that they are dirty, run-down or unsafe. That is too simplistic. Some established communities have mature trees, larger lots, individuality, long-term neighbours and the Jamaican residential feel that some returnees are actually looking for.

Gated developments can offer newer homes, pools, gyms, landscaping, controlled entry and other conveniences. They can also experience security incidents, and the lifestyle can come with association fees and rules governing things such as exterior changes, parking, landscaping or even what colours a house may be painted.

Should I rent first or buy right away?
Renting first can give you time to learn traffic patterns, utility reliability, internet options, noise, shopping, flooding/drainage, commute times and the personality of different communities before making a long-term commitment. Buying immediately may make sense if you already know the area well, have financing or cash organised and have done appropriate property due diligence. Neither route is automatically better.
What should I tell a real-estate agent before we start?
Give them your preferred area, realistic monthly rent or purchase range, absolute maximum, number of bedrooms, must-have features, deal-breakers and whether you want gated, established, rural, town-centre or resort-style living. Ask them not to send properties above your ceiling unless you specifically request comparisons.
What should I check before renting?
Test water pressure, internet availability, mobile signal, AC units, appliances, doors, windows and security features. Ask who pays utilities and maintenance, what is included in the rent, how deposits are handled, whether there are community fees or rules, and what happens when something breaks. Photograph the condition of the property when you move in.
What should I check before buying?
Beyond the appearance of the house, investigate title and legal ownership, boundaries/survey information, property taxes, community or maintenance fees, access roads, drainage/flooding, water storage, electricity, internet, structural condition, insurance considerations and any restrictions that affect how you can use or alter the property. Use qualified Jamaican legal and property professionals for the formal checks.
How should I compare gated and ungated communities?
Visit at different times of day. Talk to people who actually live there. Look at lighting, pedestrian activity, road condition, drainage, access to shops and transport, noise, trees and shade, lot size, privacy, community rules and actual security practices. If you are looking for mature trees and that real Jamaican feel, a gated, resort-style community with cookie-cutter houses and an association telling you what colour you can paint your house may not be it. On the other hand, if controlled entry, newer amenities and a more uniform community are what make you comfortable, that may be exactly what you want.
Accent, belonging + coming home
“I know when to speak patois. Having to perform it to be respected is a different issue.”
🎧 A real conversation from my return

Coming home should not require proving that you are Jamaican.

One of the more unexpected parts of returning has been thinking about when I speak patois, when I use Standard English, and how people sometimes respond to the accent I brought home with me.

In this private conversation, a local driver was advising me about situations in which speaking patois may change how people respond to me. I understand the practical point. I know how to speak patois and I know when I naturally use it. What sits differently with me is the idea that I should have to switch how I speak in order to receive fair treatment, a fair price or basic respect.

My perspective: Jamaicans who remained on the island are not unfamiliar with Jamaicans leaving, living “foreign,” and returning with a different accent or a little twang. That does not make us less Jamaican. A returning resident should not have to cuss someone out, exaggerate patois, or prove cultural belonging before being treated properly.
Why this matters for returning residents
You may notice that people read your accent before they know your story. Sometimes code-switching is simply natural communication. Sometimes it can feel like pressure to perform familiarity so that you are not overcharged, dismissed or treated as an outsider. Learn the local rhythms, ask what things normally cost, and build relationships — but do not feel that you have to erase the person you became while living abroad in order to belong at home.

07 Actually living here

Build a life — not just a successful move.

Community, healthcare, transportation, security and knowing who to call matter just as much as clearing the container.

🤝

Give yourself time to learn Jamaica again. Returning home does not mean you automatically know how everything works here now. You may know the culture, the food, the language and the people, yet still have to relearn systems, prices, places and everyday ways of doing things. That does not mean you made the wrong decision. It means you are settling in.

Community

Find your people early

WhatsApp groups and local connections are useful, but choose carefully how you participate and who you trust.

Security

Create the home setup you need

Exterior lighting, cameras, secure locks and knowing your neighbours can help you feel settled without living in fear.

Mindset

Do not be scared to live

Jamaicans are living ordinary, rich, complicated lives too. Meet people. Learn your community. Be intentional.

What should I know about public healthcare?
Jamaica maintains a no-user-fee policy for qualifying public patients in public facilities. That does not mean every medicine, test or private service is automatically free. Many people still keep private insurance or an emergency medical fund.
Should I apply for NIS?
NIS is Jamaica’s contributory social-security programme. If you are working or self-employed, check whether registration applies to your situation and complete it early.
What about a Jamaican driving licence?
Do not assume one rule fits every returning resident. Requirements can depend on your existing licence, status, insurance and the transaction you are completing. Confirm current requirements with the Island Traffic Authority / Tax Administration. If you need a provisional licence, prepare the required photographs, medical certificate, identity/residence evidence and TRN.
What should I prepare for hurricane season?
Keep water, flashlights, charged battery banks, basic medicines, important phone numbers and a modest cash reserve. If your work depends on internet, test your backup data and power arrangements before a storm is approaching.

The question underneath all the checklists
“Can I actually make a life in Jamaica?”
My answer so far

I think I can. I am learning how.

If you are reading pages like this because you are thinking about coming home, I know you are probably not only looking for Customs forms and phone numbers. You are trying to imagine yourself here.

You want to know what happens when the excitement of moving wears off. Will you find your people? Can you get things done? Will you feel safe? Can you afford to live? Will people help you? Will you regret it?

I cannot answer all of that for you because your experience will be your own. I can tell you that mine has been a mixture. Some processes have frustrated me. Some things have cost more than I expected. I have had moments of wondering what on earth I was doing.

I have also met people who have freely shared information, made introductions, helped me solve problems and encouraged what I am trying to build. That matters. I have been able to create connections here remarkably quickly.

So this is where I am right now: I do not think the question is whether Jamaica can be exactly like the life I had somewhere else. It cannot, and I do not need it to be. The question for me is whether I can learn how to build a good life here. So far, I believe I can.
