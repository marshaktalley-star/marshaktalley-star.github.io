Why I am offering this support

Special Education should be more than a placement.

In this short video, I share who I am, what I have seen across education, and why I am making my Special Education experience available directly to learners, families and educators.

I have spent more than 20 years in education. One thing I know for sure is this: students with Special Educational Needs can and do learn. They may learn at a different pace. They may need information presented differently. They may need different tools, more time, more structure, or a different route into the learning. That does not mean they cannot learn.

With the right tools, resources, teaching and expectations, students with Special Needs can excel. They can work, create, contribute, lead and be productive members of society. We should be preparing them for possibility, not deciding their limits for them.

Special Education & Behaviour in Practice

Look beyond the behaviour.

A learner who refuses to work, leaves their seat or appears disengaged may need more than firmer behaviour management. Sometimes we need to ask what may be preventing that learner from accessing the learning.

Understanding behaviour is only the beginning. Differentiating instruction, breaking tasks into manageable steps and providing appropriate supports can help learners move from frustration and avoidance toward greater independence and engagement.

The academic expectation does not have to disappear. How the learner accesses learning may need to change.

Classroom Ecology

The environment is part of the learning.

What we teach matters. How we teach matters. The environment in which students are expected to learn matters too.

Create a space students want to be in.

That can mean thinking about lighting, noise, seating, movement, clutter, colour and even something as simple as adding plants. A classroom can either help a learner settle, engage and participate, or make learning harder before the lesson even begins.

This matters for every learner. It can matter even more for students with sensory, attention, regulation or communication needs. Differentiation is not only what is on the worksheet. Sometimes it is what we change in the environment so the student can access the learning.

Start Here

Being in the room does not mean the student is included.

A student can sit in a mainstream classroom all day and still have very little access to the learning taking place. Differentiation asks us to examine the barriers between the learner and the lesson and intentionally remove or reduce them.

Differentiation does not mean lowering expectations. It means creating different pathways through which learners can access, engage with and demonstrate learning.
Think about a learner. Consider a student you have taught, observed or supported who was physically present in the classroom but academically disconnected. What appeared to be getting in the way?
Think Differently

Begin with the challenges, not the label.

A diagnosis can help us understand a learner, but it is not the be all end all. Two students with the same identified need may require very different support.

1. Learner What strengths, needs, interests and prior knowledge does this learner bring?
2. Task What exactly are we asking the learner to understand or do?
3. Barrier What may be making the task unnecessarily difficult to access?
4. Adjustment What can we change without removing the purpose of the learning?
Quick Check

What would you do first?

Scenario 1

A student repeatedly puts their head on the desk when a long written task begins. The student participates verbally and appears to understand the discussion.

Yes. Start by investigating the barrier. The learner may understand the content while struggling with the way the task requires them to access or demonstrate that understanding.
Consider what the behaviour may be communicating before assuming that the problem is motivation or deliberate refusal.
Scenario 2

A learner with attention and regulation difficulties becomes increasingly restless during a long teacher-led explanation.

Exactly. Adjusting the structure of instruction can support attention and regulation without removing the learning expectation.
Think about whether the structure of the lesson is helping or increasing the learner's difficulty with regulation.
Classroom Practice

Small adjustments can change access.

Chunk instructions Give one manageable step at a time rather than several directions at once.
Use visual support Pair spoken information with visual cues, examples, models or a simple sequence.
Check understanding Ask the learner to show or explain what they understand rather than relying only on “Do you understand?”
Build in movement Consider whether purposeful movement can support attention and regulation rather than treating all movement as disruption.
Reduce unnecessary load Ask which parts of the task measure the intended learning and which parts may simply be creating additional barriers.
Offer ways to respond Where appropriate, allow learners to demonstrate understanding through speaking, writing, visuals, technology or practical work.
Behaviour as Communication

Ask what may be underneath what you can see.

What we see

“The student won't start.”

Before deciding the student is simply refusing, consider whether they understand the task, know how to begin, can manage the amount of information presented, or fear getting it wrong.

What we see

“The student keeps leaving the seat.”

Consider attention, regulation, sensory needs, task difficulty, the length of instruction, movement needs and what happens immediately before the student gets up.

What we see

“The student shuts down.”

Withdrawal may communicate overwhelm, anxiety, confusion, exhaustion or difficulty processing what is happening.

What we see

“The student is disruptive.”

Behaviour still needs boundaries, but effective support also asks what is contributing to the behaviour and what can be changed before escalation occurs.

Regulate rather than escalate. The goal is not to excuse harmful behaviour. It is to respond in a way that protects dignity, maintains appropriate boundaries and increases the learner's ability to return to learning.

Frequently asked questions

Not sure what kind of support you need?

You do not need to diagnose the problem before contacting me. Start with what you are seeing.

Do you diagnose ADHD, autism, dyslexia or other conditions?

No. I provide educational support, consultation, training and practical strategies. Formal diagnosis or psychological assessment should be completed by an appropriately qualified professional.

Can I contact you even if my child does not have a diagnosis?

Yes. You can begin with the learning, behaviour, communication, attention or school concern you are observing. A diagnosis is not required simply to discuss educational strategies and possible next steps.

Do you work directly with schools?

Yes. Support can include workshops, professional development, presentations, consultation, in-class support and practical work around differentiation, behaviour, inclusion and classroom ecology.

Can you tailor a workshop to our school or organisation?

Yes. I prefer to understand the audience, current challenges, desired outcomes and available time before finalising a session. That is why the enquiry form asks a few qualifying questions rather than offering one generic workshop.

Can sessions be delivered virtually?

Yes. Depending on the type of support, sessions can be delivered in person, virtually or through a hybrid model.

Is your work only for teachers?

No. I work with learners, parents, teachers, school leaders and organisations. The support is adapted to the people involved and what they need to understand or do differently.

What do you mean by classroom ecology?

Classroom ecology looks at how the environment itself affects behaviour, participation and learning. That can include lighting, noise, seating, movement, relationships, routines, sensory demands, instructional structure and the overall experience of being in the room.

I keep it grounded in the classroom: what can the teacher actually do with the students in front of them?
