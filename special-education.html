Students with Special Needs Can Learn.

I support parents, students, teachers and schools to help students access the best learning for them.

Why I am offering this support

Special Education should be more than a placement.

In this short video, I share who I am, what I have seen across education, and why I am now making my Special Education experience available directly to families, students and educators.

I have spent more than 20 years in education. One thing I know for sure is this: students with Special Educational Needs can and do learn. They may learn at a different pace. They may need information presented differently. They may need different tools, more time, more structure, or a different route into the learning. That does not mean they cannot learn.

With the right tools, resources, teaching and expectations, students with Special Needs can excel. They can work, create, contribute, lead and be productive members of society. We should be preparing them for possibility, not deciding their limits for them.

Classroom Ecology

The environment is part of the learning.

I talk a lot about what we teach and how we teach it, but I also pay attention to the space itself. A classroom can either help a student settle, engage and participate, or make learning harder before the lesson even begins.

Create a space students want to be in.

That can mean thinking about lighting, noise, seating, movement, clutter, colour and even something as simple as adding plants. When students feel comfortable in the space, when the room feels cared for and they feel considered in it, they are more likely to want to be there. Once they want to be there, they are more likely to be willing to learn.

This matters for every learner. It can matter even more for students with sensory, attention, regulation or communication needs. Differentiation is not only what is on the worksheet. Sometimes it is what we change in the environment so the student can access the learning in the first place.

Start Here

Being in the room does not mean the student is included.

A student can sit in a mainstream classroom all day and still have very little access to the learning taking place. Differentiation asks us to examine the barriers between the learner and the lesson, and intentionally remove or reduce them.

Differentiation does not mean lowering expectations. It means creating different pathways through which learners can access, engage with and demonstrate learning.
Talk first. Think about a student you have taught, observed or supported who was physically present in the classroom but academically disconnected. What was happening?
Think Differently

Begin with the barrier, not the label.

A diagnosis can help us understand a learner, but it should never become the entire explanation for that learner. Two students with the same identified need may require very different support.

1. Learner What strengths, needs, interests and prior knowledge does this learner bring?
2. Task What exactly are we asking the student to understand or do?
3. Barrier What may be preventing meaningful access or participation?
4. Adjustment What can we change without removing the core learning goal?
Case Study 1

Dyslexia: When reading becomes the barrier

Meet Andre

Andre knows more than his written work suggests.

Andre is 13. During class discussions he demonstrates strong understanding and often makes thoughtful connections. When students are asked to read a long passage independently and answer written questions, however, he works slowly, skips words and sometimes stops altogether.

A teacher tells him, “You need to try harder. Everyone else has finished.”

Decision 1
Which response provides the strongest starting point for differentiation?
Yes. The goal is not simply to remove the task. Determine the barrier and preserve access to the important learning.
Consider whether this response changes the barrier or simply removes Andre from the learning.
Your turn: The learning goal is understanding the causes of a historical event. Reading fluency is not the skill being assessed. Name two ways Andre could access the content.
Case Study 2

Autism: Look beneath the behaviour

Meet Kayla

A classroom change creates a sudden response.

Kayla usually participates well when the class follows a predictable routine. Today the teacher unexpectedly changes the seating plan, introduces a group task and turns on a video while students are talking loudly.

Kayla covers her ears, refuses to join her group and repeatedly says, “No.”

Decision 2
What should the teacher consider before interpreting Kayla's response as deliberate defiance?
Correct. Behaviour provides information. Context matters before we decide what a student's behaviour means.
Look at what changed in the environment immediately before the behaviour.
Discuss aloud: What could the teacher have done before the activity to increase predictability without excluding Kayla from collaborative learning?
Case Study 3

ADHD & executive function: “He knows it,but doesn't do it.”

Meet Daniel

Daniel frequently begins assignments but does not finish them. His desk contains unfinished papers. During long teacher explanations he begins tapping, looking around the room and talking to peers. When given a complicated six-step task, he completes the first instruction and then asks, “What am I supposed to do?”

Decision 3
Which adjustment most directly addresses the demands described?
Exactly. The support reduces executive-function demands while preserving the academic expectation.
Ask yourself which response provides structure without reducing the learning expectation.
Case Study 4

Behaviour: What happened before the disruption?

Meet Malik

Malik regularly calls out, walks around the classroom and jokes with other students during independent written work. Staff describe him as disruptive.

During practical activities, demonstrations and class debates, however, Malik is engaged and frequently contributes useful ideas.

Decision 4
Which question would provide the most useful information?
Yes. Looking at antecedents, task demands, context and consequences gives us far more useful information than the label “disruptive.”
Behaviour itself is only part of the information. Look at the whole classroom situation.

Strategy Bank

Different pathways. Shared learning.

Open each section during the workshop and ask participants which strategies could be used immediately in their own classrooms.

Dyslexia & literacy-related barriers
Consider text-to-speech or audio access where appropriate, shorter chunks of text, explicit vocabulary instruction, visual support, structured literacy approaches, additional processing time, alternatives to unnecessary copying, and opportunities to demonstrate understanding orally when writing is not the skill being assessed.
Autism & differences in communication, sensory processing and predictability
Consider visual schedules, advance warning of changes, explicit instructions, reduced ambiguity, sensory-aware environments, structured choices, processing time, predictable routines and multiple ways to participate. Support should be individualized rather than based solely on diagnosis.
ADHD, attention & executive-function needs
Consider visible steps, chunked assignments, timers, checklists, purposeful movement, frequent feedback, reduced unnecessary distractions, shorter instructional segments, organizational supports and opportunities for active participation.
Behaviour, regulation & emotional needs
Consider antecedents, relationship, task difficulty, boredom, communication, regulation, environmental stressors, reinforcement, choice and opportunities for success. Ask what the behaviour may be communicating before relying solely on consequences.
Quick Check

Myth or accurate?

“Differentiation means creating an entirely different lesson for every student.”
Correct. Effective differentiation often involves adjusting access, support, grouping, pacing, materials, response options or the learning environment,not writing thirty separate lessons.
Think about whether one lesson can contain several pathways to the same important learning.

Special Education Quick Guide

What are you trying to understand?

Parents and teachers often know something is not working before they know what to call it. Start with the question that sounds closest to what you are dealing with.

My child is struggling at school. Does that automatically mean Special Education?
No. Struggling does not automatically mean a child belongs in Special Education. I want to know what the student can do, where the difficulty shows up, when it changes, and what has already been tried. We begin with the learner, not a label.
Can you diagnose autism, ADHD or dyslexia?
No. I provide educational and behaviour support, not clinical diagnosis. I can help you make sense of what you are seeing, understand how it may be affecting learning and decide whether a formal assessment by an appropriately qualified professional would be useful.
My child already has a diagnosis. What happens next?
A diagnosis gives us information. It does not tell us everything about the child and it is not an education plan by itself. We still need to ask: What can this learner do? What is getting in the way? What support, tools, teaching and environment will help this student learn and make progress?
My child is being described as disruptive, lazy or difficult. Can you help?
Yes. Behaviour gives us information. I want to know what happens before the behaviour, what the student is being asked to do, whether the work is too difficult or too easy, whether boredom is part of it, and what happens afterwards. The goal is to regulate rather than escalate and to understand before we simply punish.
Do you offer ordinary tutoring?
Yes, I offer private tutoring and learning support. I am not interested in sitting a struggling student down and giving them more of the same thing that is already not working. I adapt the support to the learner's strengths, pace, attention, confidence and the way they access learning best.
Can you train my teachers or speak to a school team?
Yes. I work with teachers and school teams on differentiation, inclusion, behaviour, classroom ecology, autism, ADHD, dyslexia, regulation and practical strategies. I keep it grounded in the classroom. What can the teacher actually do tomorrow with the students in front of them? You can also read my Classroom Ecology article for more on how the environment itself affects participation, regulation and learning.
